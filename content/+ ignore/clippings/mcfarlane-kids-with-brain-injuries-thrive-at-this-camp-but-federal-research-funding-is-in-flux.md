> The existence of Camp Cranium, as well as a handful of others like it, is a response to a decades-long national trend: More people, including children, now survive crises resulting in brain injury than they did in the 1980s. The improvements in survival are largely due to seat belt laws and [federal investments in research](https://www.congress.gov/bill/101st-congress/house-bill/1602) and trauma centers that can treat injuries quickly.

Programs like Camp Cranium are created to serve the needs of a growing population of TBI survivors.

Increased survival/prevalence rate is true for people with disabilities in general. We have more legislation, medical knowledge, technology, and care programs that support people with disabilities than we did 40 years ago.

> In 2018, a CDC team piloted a household survey asking about TBIs in a sample of U.S. children and adults. The results [informed a study](https://www.ovid.com/jnls/headtraumarehab/fulltext/10.1097/htr.0000000000000975~summary-of-the-centers-for-disease-control-and-preventions) concluding that such injuries, which are often considered "hidden" because the damage is internal and unseen, are more widespread than hospitalization numbers suggest.

Reported prevalence rates are based on data from TBI hospital admissions and diagnostic testing. People with hidden disabilities could get overlooked. Reporting efforts are also dependent on *money*.
